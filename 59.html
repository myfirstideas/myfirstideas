Ketamine for Depression and Mental Health: What You Need to Know

Ketamine has moved from the operating room to the psychiatrist's office. Originally developed as an anesthetic, it's now used off-label to treat depression, particularly when other medications haven't worked.

How Ketamine Works

Unlike traditional antidepressants that increase serotonin levels, ketamine works through a different mechanism. It blocks a receptor in the brain called NMDA, which may reset neural connections and provide rapid symptom relief in some people.

What the Evidence Shows

Ketamine works faster than conventional antidepressants. Some people report mood improvement within hours or days rather than weeks. This speed makes it valuable for people in crisis or experiencing severe depression.

It's most commonly used for treatment-resistant depression (TRD), meaning depression that hasn't responded to at least two other antidepressants.

How It's Administered

Ketamine for mental health is typically given as:

  • Intravenous (IV) infusions in a clinic setting
  • Intranasal spray (esketamine or Spravato)
  • Oral tablets (off-label use)

IV treatment usually involves a series of sessions over several weeks, with effects monitored by a healthcare provider.

Cost Considerations

Ketamine treatment isn't cheap. IV sessions can cost $300 to $1,000 per infusion before insurance. Esketamine (Spravato) is FDA-approved but often requires significant out-of-pocket costs if your insurance doesn't cover it. Check your plan's coverage before starting treatment.

Side Effects and Risks

Common side effects include dissociation (feeling detached from reality), dizziness, and nausea. These typically fade quickly. Long-term risks and dependency potential are still being studied, particularly with frequent use.

Finding Ketamine Treatment

Ketamine for mental health is available through specialized clinics, some psychiatrist offices, and telemedicine providers. Not all primary care doctors offer it. Make sure any provider you see is licensed and experienced with ketamine protocols.

The Bottom Line

Ketamine offers hope for people with severe, treatment-resistant depression when other options have failed. The evidence for rapid effect is solid, but it's not a first-line treatment and shouldn't be your first choice. It works best as part of a broader treatment plan that includes therapy and ongoing psychiatric care.
