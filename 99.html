Tretinoin is a prescription-strength vitamin A derivative that tackles acne and signs of skin aging. It's more potent than over-the-counter retinol products, which is why it requires a doctor's approval.

How Tretinoin Works

Tretinoin increases skin cell turnover and reduces sebum production. This clears blocked pores that trap bacteria and cause acne, and it also stimulates collagen production to smooth fine lines and improve skin texture over time.

Starting Tretinoin: What to Expect

Your skin will likely get worse before it improves. This "retinization" phase lasts 6 to 12 weeks and involves redness, flaking, and increased breakouts. This is normal; your skin is adjusting.

Results appear gradually. Acne improvement takes 8 to 12 weeks. Anti-aging benefits take 3 to 6 months or longer.

Common Side Effects

During the adjustment phase:

  • Redness and irritation
  • Dryness and peeling
  • Increased sensitivity to sun
  • Temporary worsening of acne

Side effects usually fade once your skin acclimates.

How to Use It Safely

Start low and go slow. Most people begin with 0.025% strength, used 2 to 3 times per week at night. Increase frequency and strength only after your skin tolerates it.

Key rules:

  • Apply to clean, completely dry skin
  • Use a pea-sized amount
  • Wait 15 to 20 minutes after cleansing before applying
  • Wear SPF 30 or higher every day; tretinoin makes skin sun-sensitive
  • Avoid other potentially irritating actives initially (vitamin C, AHAs, BHAs, niacinamide)
  • Do not use if pregnant or breastfeeding

Who Should Not Use Tretinoin

Avoid tretinoin if you are pregnant, planning to become pregnant, or breastfeeding. It's also not suitable if you have eczema or severe rosacea without medical supervision.

Getting a Tretinoin Prescription

You'll need a prescription from a dermatologist or primary care doctor. Telehealth and telemedicine services can prescribe tretinoin after an online consultation, making it more accessible than in-office visits alone.
