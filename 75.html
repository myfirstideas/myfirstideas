How oral contraceptives work

Oral contraceptives, also called birth control pills, prevent pregnancy through hormones. Most combine estrogen and progestin, though some contain progestin only.

The hormones stop your ovaries from releasing an egg each month. They also thicken cervical mucus to block sperm, and thin the uterine lining. This triple action makes the pill highly effective when taken consistently.

Effectiveness

Oral contraceptives are 91 to 99 percent effective at preventing pregnancy, depending on how consistently you take them. Missing pills or taking them at irregular times lowers effectiveness.

Common side effects

Many people experience side effects in the first few months, which often improve:

  • Nausea
  • Breast tenderness
  • Headaches
  • Spotting or breakthrough bleeding
  • Mood changes
  • Bloating

Side effects usually settle within 2 to 3 months. If they persist, talk to your clinician about switching to a different formulation.

What to know before starting

Oral contraceptives require a prescription. Your clinician will review your health history, current medications, and any conditions that might affect safety.

Some people should not take oral contraceptives, including those with a history of blood clots, certain types of migraine, or uncontrolled high blood pressure.

Oral contraceptives do not protect against sexually transmitted infections. Use condoms for STI protection.

Getting started

You can obtain oral contraceptives through in-person visits with your primary care doctor, gynaecologist, or through telehealth appointments. Many telehealth providers offer prescription refills and can answer questions about side effects or switching formulations.

Start dates and pill packs vary. Your clinician will walk you through timing and what to expect.
