Testosterone pellets are small cylinders inserted under the skin to deliver steady hormone doses over months. For women, they're marketed for energy, libido, and muscle tone, but the evidence is mixed and the risks are concrete.

How testosterone pellets work for women

Unlike pills or creams that fluctuate in your bloodstream, pellets release a steady stream of hormone. They're implanted in the fatty layer under your skin, usually on the hip or buttock, in a 10-minute office procedure. Most last 3 to 6 months depending on dose and individual metabolism.

Real costs and what's covered

Pellet insertion typically runs 300 to 600 dollars per procedure. Removal, if you need it, costs another 200 to 400 dollars. Insurance rarely covers this, so most people pay out of pocket. Stacking up costs over a year makes pellets more expensive than creams or patches at equivalent doses.

Dosing: what's standard and what's not

Women's testosterone pellets usually start at 25 to 50 mg. Some clinics go higher, up to 75 mg. Higher doses don't necessarily mean better results. A dose that works depends on your baseline hormone levels, age, weight, and symptoms. Reputable clinics order pre-treatment bloodwork and adjust based on results, not guesses.

Risks you should weigh

Testosterone can deepen your voice permanently, increase body hair and facial hair, cause acne, and shift cholesterol patterns. Some women report mood changes, anxiety, or aggression. Pellet complications include infection at the insertion site, extrusion (pellet pushing out), and inability to remove all of it if you want to stop. Blood clots are rare but possible. Long-term cardiovascular and cancer risks in women using testosterone are not well studied.

How to vet a clinic

Look for these signs of a responsible operator:

  • Pre-treatment bloodwork including lipids, glucose, and baseline testosterone
  • Baseline cardiovascular assessment, especially if you have risk factors
  • Documented informed consent that names real risks, not just benefits
  • Clear pricing that includes removal costs upfront
  • Follow-up bloodwork at 6 weeks and 3 months
  • Willingness to lower dose or remove pellets if you have side effects
  • Licensed physician performing insertion, not a nurse or technician
  • Ability to answer questions about their complication rates

Clinics that promise mood boosts, sexual performance, or muscle gains without mentioning voice deepening, hair growth, or cardiovascular unknowns are cutting corners on informed consent.

The bottom line

Testosterone pellets can help some women feel more energized or interested in sex. They're not a shortcut. They cost more than alternatives, carry permanent and reversible risks, and require active management. If you choose pellets, pick a clinic that treats them like medicine, not like a wellness upgrade.
